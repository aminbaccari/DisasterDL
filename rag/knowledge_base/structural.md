# Structural and urban emergencies knowledge base

Knowledge for structural and urban emergencies. Sources: FEMA P-2055 and FEMA P-2055-2 (English, United States). They are written for building safety evaluators and emergency managers, and the cordon material is written for earthquakes. Entries marked professional are context for the assistant, not instructions for the public.

Entries: 26. Languages: en. Each entry is self-contained and ends with its source and PDF page.

## ST-01 Who decides: building safety evaluators, not responders or software

Deciding whether a damaged building is safe, and how large a cordon should be, is a task for trained and certified post-disaster building safety evaluators. The cordon recommendations assume that the evaluator has the necessary certification and training. Suggested personnel for rapid evaluations include structural engineers, engineers specialised in structures, architects, building officials, inspectors and experienced contractors.

Source: FEMA P-2055-2, Recommendations for Cordoning Earthquake-Damaged Buildings (2023, 2nd issuance 2025), PDF page 15; FEMA P-2055, Post-disaster Building Safety Evaluation Guidance (2019), PDF page 29.
Origin of the sources: US. Audience: public. Type: taken from the sources.

## ST-02 Levels of evaluation: rapid, detailed, engineering

Post-disaster building evaluation has levels. A rapid evaluation takes an average of 30 minutes per building and gives an initial general evaluation of damage and safety, to quickly identify and post unsafe and apparently safe structures. A detailed evaluation takes one to four hours per building and is a more thorough visual examination of the building and its structural system. An engineering evaluation is a detailed investigation by consultants, hired by the owner, using drawings, damage data and new calculations. A windshield survey before the teams go out only maps the extent of damage and normally does not post placards.

Source: FEMA P-2055, Post-disaster Building Safety Evaluation Guidance (2019), PDF pages 29, 30.
Origin of the sources: US. Audience: professional. Type: taken from the sources.

## ST-03 Placards: green INSPECTED, yellow RESTRICTED USE, red UNSAFE

Damaged buildings are posted with placards after evaluation: INSPECTED (green placard, lawful permitted use), RESTRICTED USE (yellow placard, use limited) or UNSAFE (red placard, occupancy prohibited). Even when the structure is not significantly damaged, a building can be posted RESTRICTED USE or UNSAFE because of mold contamination, blocked exits or toxic chemical spills. A placard cannot be removed or changed until the hazard has been mitigated.

Source: FEMA P-2055, Post-disaster Building Safety Evaluation Guidance (2019), PDF pages 29, 31; FEMA P-2055-2, Recommendations for Cordoning Earthquake-Damaged Buildings (2023, 2nd issuance 2025), PDF pages 18, 61.
Origin of the sources: US. Audience: public. Type: taken from the sources.

## ST-04 Rapid evaluation criteria and the posting action for each

Rapid evaluation criteria after an earthquake, with the action for severe cases: building collapsed, partially collapsed or moved off its foundation, post UNSAFE. Building or any story significantly out of plumb (leaning), post UNSAFE. Obvious severe damage to primary structural members or severe racking of walls, post UNSAFE. Obvious parapet, chimney or other falling hazard, post RESTRICTED USE and barricade the unsafe area. Large ground fissures, massive ground movement or slope displacement, post UNSAFE. Other hazard such as a toxic spill, asbestos contamination, a broken gas line or a fallen power line, post UNSAFE and/or barricade the unsafe area.

Source: FEMA P-2055, Post-disaster Building Safety Evaluation Guidance (2019), PDF page 49.
Origin of the sources: US. Audience: professional. Type: taken from the sources.

## ST-05 Do not enter obviously unsafe buildings; entry rules

Entering damaged buildings: do not enter obviously unsafe buildings. A building should not be entered without the permission of the owner or occupant, unless authorized by the local jurisdiction. Most rapid evaluations are mainly exterior inspections, and evaluators try to enter only when interior damage is suspected or visible from the outside, when not enough of the structure can be seen from the outside, or to talk with the manager or occupants.

Source: FEMA P-2055, Post-disaster Building Safety Evaluation Guidance (2019), PDF page 29.
Origin of the sources: US. Audience: public. Type: taken from the sources.

## ST-06 Purpose of a cordon and why aftershocks matter

A safety cordon evacuates the area around at-risk buildings and restricts access to damaged and partially collapsed buildings. Its purpose is to control public access, to prevent further casualties in a large aftershock, and to isolate damaged buildings in a restricted perimeter where authorized personnel can repair, demolish or rebuild without harming people. Large aftershocks have caused much of the death toll in past earthquake sequences, because buildings already damaged by the main shock collapse.

Source: FEMA P-2055-2, Recommendations for Cordoning Earthquake-Damaged Buildings (2023, 2nd issuance 2025), PDF page 17.
Origin of the sources: US. Audience: public. Type: taken from the sources.

## ST-07 Initial cordon distance: 1.5 times height versus height above the damage point

Initial cordon distance around a damaged building: the earlier California guidance and fire-service practice set a soft barrier at a horizontal distance of up to 1.5 times the building height (1.5H:1V), because falling items can bounce and shatter. FEMA P-2055-2 says this is logical and acceptable for common fire emergencies in low-rise buildings of three stories or less, but overly conservative, particularly for taller buildings, where it could create an exclusion zone up to one-half mile in diameter. For a building with obvious serious damage or a visible lean, it recommends an initial cordon distance D equal to the height of the building above the significant damage or hinge point, then refining it. The sources give no distance for a tall building whose damage point is unknown. Any wider default cordon is temporary until evaluators can justify a shorter distance.

Source: FEMA P-2055, Post-disaster Building Safety Evaluation Guidance (2019), PDF page 179; FEMA P-2055-2, Recommendations for Cordoning Earthquake-Damaged Buildings (2023, 2nd issuance 2025), PDF pages 18, 24.

## ST-08 Wide cordons until evaluators can shorten them

Wide safe distances, including block-long cordons, are warranted until inspectors, evaluators, owners, engineers and contractors can evaluate, stabilize or remove potential falling or collapse risks and erect hard, impact-resistant barriers to protect the public. The wider initial distance is meant to be replaced by shorter safe distances once the nature and extent of building damage has been investigated and shorter distances can be justified.

Source: FEMA P-2055, Post-disaster Building Safety Evaluation Guidance (2019), PDF page 179; FEMA P-2055-2, Recommendations for Cordoning Earthquake-Damaged Buildings (2023, 2nd issuance 2025), PDF page 18.
Origin of the sources: US. Audience: professional. Type: taken from the sources.

## ST-09 Collapse shadow: a wedge, not a full circle

A collapse shadow cordon is a wedge or a limited rectangle in the direction a building may fall, not a default 360-degree circle, and this greatly reduces the impact of the cordon on recovery. It depends on the likely collapse mode, which depends on the damage, the structure type and the stability of the damaged building. Every building damaged enough to threaten collapse has a fatal flaw that the earthquake exposed, such as unreinforced masonry, non-ductile concrete, a weak story or a torsional irregularity. Cordon area determination relies on a realistic engineering assessment of the stability and potential collapse of the building.

Source: FEMA P-2055-2, Recommendations for Cordoning Earthquake-Damaged Buildings (2023, 2nd issuance 2025), PDF pages 17, 24.
Origin of the sources: US. Audience: professional. Type: taken from the sources.

## ST-10 Collapse shadow by collapse mode, part 1 (overturning, story, sidesway, torsion)

Collapse shadow cordons by visible damage and collapse mode. Monolithic overturning (visible lean of a ductile concrete core or braced frame): 45-degree wedge along the direction of tilt, distance D equal to the height of the building above the hinge point. Frame with a story mechanism where progressive collapse is unlikely: rectangle as wide as the building, D equal to the story height. Frame with a sidesway mechanism where progressive collapse is likely: 45-degree wedge along the tilt, D equal to the building height. Frame with a stable story mechanism: minimal collapse shadow. Torsional damage: 45-degree wedge, D equal to the height above the hinge point.

Source: FEMA P-2055-2, Recommendations for Cordoning Earthquake-Damaged Buildings (2023, 2nd issuance 2025), PDF pages 25, 26, 27.
Origin of the sources: US. Audience: professional. Type: taken from the sources.

## ST-11 Collapse shadow by collapse mode, part 2 (pancake, wall fall, rubble pile)

Collapse shadow cordons by collapse mode, continued. Pancake collapse of flat concrete slabs with unbraced remaining columns or walls: circle with radius D equal to the height of the unsupported vertical elements. Tilt-up or other facade panel systems with damaged wall connections: rectangle from each affected wall, D equal to the height of the wall panels. Masonry walls with damaged connections to the diaphragm: 45-degree wedge, D equal to half the wall panel height, and for buildings up to 4 stories D can be approximated as the building height. Interior column failure with inward tilt of the perimeter: minimal collapse shadow. Non-engineered masonry, adobe or block buildings partly collapsed as a rubble pile: rectangle from each damaged wall, D equal to 0.15 H.

Source: FEMA P-2055-2, Recommendations for Cordoning Earthquake-Damaged Buildings (2023, 2nd issuance 2025), PDF pages 27, 28, 29.
Origin of the sources: US. Audience: professional. Type: taken from the sources.

## ST-12 Falling debris cordons (facade, parapets, chimneys)

Debris shadow cordons: small falling debris such as masonry and glass endangers people and vehicles, and larger debris such as parapets, chimneys, infill walls, curtain walls or cladding can damage adjacent buildings. Set a cordon for buildings with serious visible facade damage or that shed debris onto surfaces used by pedestrians or vehicles, and include adjacent structures when larger debris is a concern. Keep it until the hazard is mitigated by removal or restraint of damaged elements, until the structure is shown not to shed debris in aftershocks, or until the threat of aftershocks fades with time. For example, for a 100 ft building the debris distance is recommended as 12 ft.

Source: FEMA P-2055-2, Recommendations for Cordoning Earthquake-Damaged Buildings (2023, 2nd issuance 2025), PDF pages 30, 31.
Origin of the sources: US. Audience: professional. Type: taken from the sources.

## ST-13 Reducing or lifting a cordon, and re-evaluating after an aftershock

The cordon can be reduced and eventually removed only after detailed evaluation and monitoring, once shoring is completed, the stability of damaged structures against the intensity of potential aftershocks is established and the threat of further damage or collapse is mitigated. The perimeter should be re-evaluated after an aftershock. In New Zealand, bus passengers were killed in an aftershock in an area that had been inside the original cordon, after the perimeter was reduced too soon, likely under pressure to reopen the city.

Source: FEMA P-2055-2, Recommendations for Cordoning Earthquake-Damaged Buildings (2023, 2nd issuance 2025), PDF pages 24, 63.
Origin of the sources: US. Audience: professional. Type: taken from the sources.

## ST-14 When to re-evaluate: aftershocks and indicator buildings

Most natural hazard incidents have secondary events that call for re-evaluation of the affected buildings, for example a significant aftershock after an earthquake. One approach is to monitor indicator buildings that represent the local building stock: if they sustain new damage in an aftershock, similar buildings should be re-evaluated, and if not, all similar buildings need not be. If the authority is told of new damage in any building after an aftershock, that building should be evaluated.

Source: FEMA P-2055, Post-disaster Building Safety Evaluation Guidance (2019), PDF page 176.
Origin of the sources: US. Audience: professional. Type: taken from the sources.

## ST-15 Leaning buildings and soft-story collapse: aftershocks can finish the job

A leaning story may come to rest in a marginally stable state after an earthquake, and aftershocks can increase the tilt until the destabilizing force exceeds the remaining strength and the story collapses. Several weak stories in one building can collapse in cascade, and this potential cascading collapse needs a detailed engineering evaluation to inform cordon decisions. The vast majority of weak or soft story collapses occur during the main shock.

Source: FEMA P-2055-2, Recommendations for Cordoning Earthquake-Damaged Buildings (2023, 2nd issuance 2025), PDF pages 39, 40.
Origin of the sources: US. Audience: professional. Type: taken from the sources.

## ST-16 Undamaged buildings next to a damaged one

A building leaning over an adjacent building may lead the authority to post the adjacent building UNSAFE or RESTRICTED USE even if it is undamaged, depending on whether the entire building or only part of it is exposed. The placards cannot be removed or changed until the hazard is mitigated. Hazards from adjacent damaged buildings are not explicitly addressed in the basic evaluation manual, except for landslide or rockfall debris from off-site.

Source: FEMA P-2055-2, Recommendations for Cordoning Earthquake-Damaged Buildings (2023, 2nd issuance 2025), PDF pages 18, 61.
Origin of the sources: US. Audience: professional. Type: taken from the sources.

## ST-17 Gas leak in or near a damaged building

Gas leaks pose a serious threat of explosion and fire and should be dealt with immediately. Leak indicators include hissing or blowing noises, dirt thrown into the air, fire from underground, and water blowing into the air at a pond, creek or river. If you smell natural gas, leave the building and notify the fire department, the utility or an authorized representative immediately, since they may turn off the gas at the meter. Do not use phones or mobile devices inside the building and avoid anything that may spark, such as starting or stopping nearby vehicles or machinery. If someone is reported missing or trapped, contact emergency personnel from outside the building.

Source: FEMA P-2055, Post-disaster Building Safety Evaluation Guidance (2019), PDF pages 85, 86.
Origin of the sources: US. Audience: public. Type: taken from the sources.

## ST-18 Chemical spill or leaking containers near a damaged building

If there is an obvious breach of containers or a solid or liquid spill, notify the fire department, barricade, and post a RESTRICTED USE or UNSAFE placard at a safe distance. A chemical release can burn skin, produce toxic or flammable vapors, injure the respiratory tract and poison through contaminated food or water. The site should be considered too: nearby industrial facilities, storage tanks and gasoline stations may hold chemicals, and the fire department or the responsible agency should be notified if a release is known.

Source: FEMA P-2055, Post-disaster Building Safety Evaluation Guidance (2019), PDF pages 87, 88.
Origin of the sources: US. Audience: professional. Type: taken from the sources.

## ST-19 Environmental hazard tiers for a damaged building

Environmental hazards in a damaged building can be ranked in tiers. High hazard: imminent danger to habitability, for example active chemical spills or carbon monoxide. Moderate hazard: potentially serious health and safety risk that can be controlled by rapid repairs or control measures, for example water intrusion. Low hazard: less than serious risk, controlled with interim measures that allow immediate occupancy. Minimal hazard: a risk that is part of everyday life, for example mice.

Source: FEMA P-2055, Post-disaster Building Safety Evaluation Guidance (2019), PDF page 85.
Origin of the sources: US. Audience: professional. Type: taken from the sources.

## ST-20 Explosion damage: what is different and what to check

Explosions produce pressures higher than typical building design loads, and close blasts cause high-intensity damage to part of the building while distant ones load the whole structure. Blast pressure can also pull horizontal members and floors upward, which many buildings are not designed for. Non-structural effects include shattered glass and debris, racked doorways, contamination, damage to mechanical, electrical and plumbing systems, and heat damage to the building envelope. Falling hazards from shattered windows and brittle veneer must be considered. Some explosions are followed by large fires and others, such as dust explosions, are not. Existing evaluation procedures can be used, since no dedicated guidance exists.

Source: FEMA P-2055, Post-disaster Building Safety Evaluation Guidance (2019), PDF pages 63, 64.
Origin of the sources: US. Audience: professional. Type: taken from the sources.

## ST-21 Secondary collapse risk differs by incident: earthquake versus fire or explosion

The chance of a second extreme loading event is not the same for every incident. After an earthquake, aftershocks justify a conservative approach and re-evaluation. After a fire or an explosion (except intentionally placed explosions), the probability of a second extreme loading event is low, so a less conservative approach to secondary events can be used in the evaluation.

Source: FEMA P-2055, Post-disaster Building Safety Evaluation Guidance (2019), PDF pages 62, 64, 176.
Origin of the sources: US. Audience: professional. Type: taken from the sources.

## ST-22 Large cordons displace people and businesses

Cordons have a cost. In Christchurch, a cordon of the central business district lasted 857 days and displaced 6,000 businesses, and 47% of buildings in the district were judged unsafe or restricted. In Santa Cruz after the 1989 Loma Prieta earthquake a 9-block cordon displaced 600 businesses for about 30 days. A cordon sized by a simple 1.5 times height rule around a very tall building can close an area up to one-half mile in diameter. Over-conservative cordons hinder recovery, but reducing a cordon too soon can also cost lives.

Source: FEMA P-2055-2, Recommendations for Cordoning Earthquake-Damaged Buildings (2023, 2nd issuance 2025), PDF pages 18, 59, 63.
Origin of the sources: US. Audience: professional. Type: taken from the sources.

## ST-23 Two-tier cordon and controlled access for owners

After the 1989 Loma Prieta earthquake, authorities set two cordons around vulnerable areas: an outer cordon that kept out the general public but allowed access for media, non-governmental organizations and healthcare, and an inner cordon with access limited to authorized workers. Later, owners and businesses were allowed to enter the cordoned area briefly to retrieve belongings, accompanied by safety personnel.

Source: FEMA P-2055-2, Recommendations for Cordoning Earthquake-Damaged Buildings (2023, 2nd issuance 2025), PDF pages 19, 60.
Origin of the sources: US. Audience: professional. Type: taken from the sources.

## ST-24 Managing a cordon: authority, communication, records

Experience in New Zealand showed that it is critical to establish clear communications quickly to affirm cordon authority for businesses, owners, residents and tenants, to eliminate confusion and support safety. Good practice includes a dedicated cordon office, a cordon manager with building official capabilities, thorough documentation of decisions, photos and persons involved, and coordination of pedestrian, vehicle and emergency vehicle routes around the cordon. The cordon should be flexible and adaptable, and communicate well and often with stakeholders.

Source: FEMA P-2055-2, Recommendations for Cordoning Earthquake-Damaged Buildings (2023, 2nd issuance 2025), PDF pages 17, 60, 61, 63.
Origin of the sources: US. Audience: professional. Type: taken from the sources.

## ST-25 Scope of the cordon guidance: written for earthquakes

The FEMA cordon guidance was written for building collapses caused by earthquakes and aftershocks. Collapses with other causes, such as the apartment building collapses in Davenport, Iowa, and Miami, Florida, also need cordoning guidance, but this document does not provide it. The collapse-mode distances were therefore not developed for a collapse with an unknown or non-seismic cause.

Source: FEMA P-2055-2, Recommendations for Cordoning Earthquake-Damaged Buildings (2023, 2nd issuance 2025), PDF page 15.
Origin of the sources: US. Audience: public. Type: taken from the sources.

## ST-26 Falling hazards: chimneys, parapets and canopies

Damaged parapets, chimneys, canopies and similar elements are falling hazards. An obvious falling hazard leads to a RESTRICTED USE placard and a barricade around the unsafe area. When a wood-frame structure has burned to the ground but its unreinforced masonry chimney remains, the authority may apply a RESTRICTED USE placard because of the falling hazard of the chimney.

Source: FEMA P-2055, Post-disaster Building Safety Evaluation Guidance (2019), PDF pages 49, 61.
Origin of the sources: US. Audience: professional. Type: taken from the sources.
