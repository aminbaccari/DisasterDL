## FI-01 Limits of this knowledge base for fire

Limits for fire incidents: this knowledge base gives general wildfire guidance from a FEMA flyer, a French government page and a news report of Tunisian civil protection recommendations, plus building and smoke information from FEMA P-2055. It does not say what to do when flames are already reaching a house or when a fire cannot be outrun, it gives no firefighting instructions, and the Tunisian source covers prevention and reporting only. For an approaching fire, follow the orders of the authorities and emergency services. When the knowledge base has no guidance for a question, say so plainly and refer to the authorities instead of inventing steps.

## FI-02 Fires affect buildings unevenly, and damage may not be visible

After a fire or wildfire, some structures can be burned so badly that they are essentially destroyed while others are untouched, simply because of the path of the fire. A fire can be limited to one building or affect a wide region. Heat can degrade structural members in ways that are not obvious: for example, the metal connector plates of wood trusses can be significantly affected by heat without any significant visible charring of the wood. Rapid evaluations have no effective method for judging intact members exposed to fire.

Source: FEMA P-2055, Post-disaster Building Safety Evaluation Guidance (2019), PDF pages 61, 62.
Origin of the sources: US. Audience: professional. Type: taken from the sources.

## FI-03 Re-entering a fire-damaged building: what evaluators look at

Going back into a house or building after a fire: the safety evaluation of a fire-damaged building asks whether the fire has weakened the vertical load-carrying or lateral force-resisting capacity of structural elements. Charred members can be assessed for section loss. If the building leans because of fire damage, the standard earthquake criteria for leaning buildings apply. No dedicated best-practice guidance exists for fire damage, and evaluators use the detailed evaluation criteria of the standard field manual in the meantime.

Source: FEMA P-2055, Post-disaster Building Safety Evaluation Guidance (2019), PDF pages 61, 62.
Origin of the sources: US. Audience: professional. Type: taken from the sources.

## FI-04 Soot, fumes and who is most at risk

Toxicity of soot and fumes near active burning depends on what is burning, wind direction and speed, and the concentration and size of the particles. Burning plastics common in household furnishings produce highly toxic soot. People with heart or lung conditions, the elderly, smokers and children may be most affected. Evaluators of burned buildings can also be exposed to latent chemical hazards from products of combustion.

Source: FEMA P-2055, Post-disaster Building Safety Evaluation Guidance (2019), PDF pages 62, 88.
Origin of the sources: US. Audience: public. Type: taken from the sources.

## FI-05 Staying indoors during a smoke episode (smoke only, not approaching flames)

Smoke exposure, not flame danger: during a smoke episode, a common advisory is to stay indoors, where people can better control their environment. Relief from smoke and heat is best when buildings have filtered air and climate control, and some communities provide public cleaner-air shelters for people whose buildings lack adequate filtration or cooling. In high heat, staying cool, hydrated and inside helps. Follow local emergency managers and public health officials on decisions to shelter in place. This advice concerns smoke, not approaching flames. If flames approach or the authorities order an evacuation, the evacuation order takes priority.

Source: FEMA P-2055, Post-disaster Building Safety Evaluation Guidance (2019), PDF page 89.

## FI-06 Active burning found by an evaluator

If active burning is found in an area where it has not yet been reported, the building safety evaluator should notify the fire department, barricade, and post a RESTRICTED USE or UNSAFE placard at a safe distance. For buildings in an area of active burning, the evaluation focuses on outdoor air quality and on the ability of the building systems to filter incoming air.

Source: FEMA P-2055, Post-disaster Building Safety Evaluation Guidance (2019), PDF page 88.
Origin of the sources: US. Audience: professional. Type: taken from the sources.

## FI-07 Fire after an explosion or an earthquake, and delayed ignition

Fire can follow another incident. Low explosives such as fuels and gunpowder typically create large fires after detonation, while high explosives such as TNT typically do not. A fire can start after an earthquake, and fires may ignite with delay because hidden mechanical or electrical faults go unnoticed until power is restored at a building. For structures affected by fire after an explosion, both the fire and the explosion criteria apply.

Source: FEMA P-2055, Post-disaster Building Safety Evaluation Guidance (2019), PDF pages 63, 65.
Origin of the sources: US. Audience: public. Type: taken from the sources.

## FI-08 Mudslides and flash floods weeks or months after a wildfire

A secondary incident can come long after the first one: mudslides and flash floods can occur weeks or months after wildfires. A secondary incident may seem to occur at the same time as the first, or not for weeks or months.

Source: FEMA P-2055, Post-disaster Building Safety Evaluation Guidance (2019), PDF page 65.
Origin of the sources: US. Audience: public. Type: taken from the sources.

## FI-09 Under a wildfire warning: get to safety right away

If you are under a wildfire warning, get to safety right away. Leave if told to do so, and leave immediately if the authorities tell you to evacuate. Listen for emergency information and alerts from your community's alerting systems. Use N95 masks to keep particles out of the air you breathe. If you are trapped, call the emergency services and give your location, but be aware that the emergency response could be delayed or impossible; turn on lights to help people find you. The emergency number in the source is for the United States and is not given here.

Source: FEMA V-1013, Be Prepared for a Wildfire (2018), PDF pages 1, 2.

## FI-10 Before a wildfire: warnings, evacuation routes and supplies

FEMA's advice for preparing before a wildfire: sign up for your community's warning system. Know your community's evacuation routes, find several ways to leave the area, drive the evacuation routes and find shelter locations, and have a plan for pets and livestock. Gather emergency supplies, including N95 respirator masks that filter out particles in the air, and keep in mind each person's specific needs, including medication and the needs of pets. Keep important documents in a fireproof safe, with password-protected digital copies, and review insurance coverage to make sure it is enough to replace your property.

Source: FEMA V-1013, Be Prepared for a Wildfire (2018), PDF page 2.
Origin of the sources: US. Audience: public. Type: taken from the sources.

## FI-11 Making a home safer from wildfire: fire-resistant zone and water source

FEMA's advice for making a home safer from wildfire: use fire-resistant materials to build, renovate or make repairs. Find an outdoor water source with a hose that can reach any area of your property. Create a fire-resistant zone that is free of leaves, debris or flammable materials for at least 30 feet from your home.

Source: FEMA V-1013, Be Prepared for a Wildfire (2018), PDF page 2.
Origin of the sources: US. Audience: public. Type: taken from the sources.

## FI-13 Leave when told, or shelter in a cleared house: how the sources fit together

Two kinds of advice need to be read together when a wildfire threatens. FEMA says to evacuate and leave immediately if the authorities tell you to do so, and to listen for emergency information and alerts. The French government page says that a person who sees a fire starting should alert the rescue services and take shelter in a house cleared of vegetation until rescuers arrive, and that a car is not a safe shelter because it could burn. Both give priority to instructions from the authorities. If the authorities order an evacuation, leave. The sources do not say when to leave rather than shelter in a cleared house, and give no further instructions for flames already reaching a house.

## FI-18 Protecting yourself from smoke: N95 mask or damp cloth

Two ways to protect yourself from smoke are given. FEMA advises using N95 masks to keep particles out of the air you breathe. The French government page advises covering the nose and mouth with a damp cloth to protect against smoke. The sources do not compare the two or say how well a damp cloth filters smoke. The mask that FEMA recommends is the N95, which is designed to keep particles out.

## FI-19 After a wildfire: when to return and what to avoid

After a wildfire, listen to the authorities to find out whether it is safe to return and whether the water is safe to drink. Avoid hot ash, charred trees, smoldering debris and live embers. The ground may contain heat pockets that can burn you or spark another fire. Consider the danger to pets and livestock walking the ground. Send text messages or use social media to reach family and friends, because phone systems are often busy after a disaster, and make calls only in emergencies. Document property damage with photographs, make an inventory and contact your insurance company.

Source: FEMA V-1013, Be Prepared for a Wildfire (2018), PDF page 2.
Origin of the sources: US. Audience: public. Type: taken from the sources.
