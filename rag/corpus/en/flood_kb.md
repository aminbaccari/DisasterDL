# Floods knowledge base

Knowledge for the flood assistant. Sources: American Red Cross checklist (English, United States), the French easy-to-read guidance Pluie et inondation (French, France) and FEMA P-2055 (English, United States, building evaluators). Phone numbers, websites and national services in the sources are not reproduced because they do not apply in Tunisia.

Entries: 19. Languages: en. Each entry is self-contained and ends with its source and PDF page.

## FL-01 What a flood is and why it kills

Flood: a temporary overflow of water onto land that is normally dry. Floods can result from rain, snow, coastal storms, storm surges, overflows of rivers and dam failure. People die by drowning when they do not evacuate before floodwaters come or when they enter floodwaters. Flooding can develop slowly or quickly, and flash floods can be sudden and violent. Floods can damage buildings and roads, cause power outages and create landslides.

Source: American Red Cross, Flood Preparedness Checklist (2022), PDF page 1.
Origin of the sources: US. Audience: public. Type: taken from the sources.

## FL-02 Before a flood: know your risk and plan a higher place to go

Flood preparation: learn which types of flooding can affect your home and community, for example flash floods, river floods, storm surges, coastal floods, debris flows, dam breaks and levee failures. Ask the local office of emergency management for advice. Flash floods may give little to no warning, so choose in advance a place on higher ground that you can reach quickly and plan to move there before flooding begins.

Source: American Red Cross, Flood Preparedness Checklist (2022), PDF page 1.
Origin of the sources: US. Audience: public. Type: taken from the sources.

## FL-03 Be ready to evacuate on official advice (rivers, storm surge, coast)

Evacuation during river floods, storm surge and coastal flooding: review your evacuation plan so you can leave quickly if officials advise you to evacuate. A storm surge can raise water levels quickly and flood large areas in just minutes, so be prepared to evacuate immediately if local officials advise. For coastal flooding, move inland before flooding begins.

Source: American Red Cross, Flood Preparedness Checklist (2022), PDF page 1.
Origin of the sources: US. Audience: public. Type: taken from the sources.

## FL-04 Never walk, swim or drive through floodwater (Red Cross figures)

Turn around, do not drown: never walk, swim or drive through floodwater. Just 6 in (15 cm) of fast-moving floodwater can knock you over, and 12 in (30 cm) can carry your vehicle away.

Source: American Red Cross, Flood Preparedness Checklist (2022), PDF page 1.
Origin of the sources: US. Audience: public. Type: taken from the sources.

## FL-12 Cutting gas and electricity during a flood: only when it is safe

Gas and electricity during a flood: the French guidance says to cut off gas, electricity and heating if possible and without putting yourself in danger. The Red Cross says not to touch electrical equipment if it is wet or if you are standing in water, because you could be electrocuted. Apply both together: switch off supplies only if you can do it safely and dry, and never while standing in water or touching wet equipment. If you smell gas, do not operate anything that may spark, leave the building and notify the fire department or the gas utility.

Source: Pluie et inondation, version FALC (Ministère français, adaptation Com'access), PDF pages 9, 10; American Red Cross, Flood Preparedness Checklist (2022), PDF page 2; FEMA P-2055, Post-disaster Building Safety Evaluation Guidance (2019), PDF page 86.

## FL-13 Stay or leave? Follow officials first, otherwise go up and do not travel

Deciding between sheltering and leaving in a flood: the sources differ in emphasis, not in substance. The Red Cross says to move to higher ground before flooding begins and to evacuate immediately if local officials advise. The French guidance says to stay or take shelter, not to travel, to go to an upper floor, never to go to a basement, and to follow the instructions of rescuers and the town hall. Both give priority to official instructions. Without instructions during sudden intense flooding with rising water, do not travel through water and move to an upper floor. The sources reviewed give no further instructions for the case where water reaches the upper floor.

Source: American Red Cross, Flood Preparedness Checklist (2022), PDF page 1; Pluie et inondation, version FALC (Ministère français, adaptation Com'access), PDF page 9.

## FL-14 Driving in floodwater: no depth is safe to assume

Driving through floodwater: the Red Cross gives figures, 6 in (15 cm) of fast-moving water can knock a person over and 12 in (30 cm) can carry a vehicle away. The French guidance says a few centimetres of water are enough to carry a car away. The figures are not identical, so no depth should be treated as safe. Both sources give the same instruction: do not drive or walk through floodwater, and postpone trips when the roads are flooded or blocked.

Source: American Red Cross, Flood Preparedness Checklist (2022), PDF page 1; Pluie et inondation, version FALC (Ministère français, adaptation Com'access), PDF page 10.

## FL-15 Staying out of floodwater: contamination

Flood contamination: stay away from floodwaters, they may contain sewage, sharp items and chemicals that can make you ill. Floodwater may carry blackwater with sewage, bacteria, viruses and parasites. Blackwater cannot be reliably identified by looking at it, so treat floodwater as contaminated if sewer or septic damage is evident, or if chemical or biological pollutants are suspected, and avoid all contact.

Source: American Red Cross, Flood Preparedness Checklist (2022), PDF page 2; FEMA P-2055, Post-disaster Building Safety Evaluation Guidance (2019), PDF pages 89, 90.
Origin of the sources: US. Audience: public. Type: taken from the sources.

## FL-16 Drinking water and food after a flood

Health after a flood: floodwater can contaminate drinking water, so check with the local public health department about drinking water safety. Throw out food that got wet or warm. When in doubt, throw it out.

Source: American Red Cross, Flood Preparedness Checklist (2022), PDF page 2.
Origin of the sources: US. Audience: public. Type: taken from the sources.

## FL-17 Returning home after evacuation, power lines and debris

After a flood: if you evacuated, wait for officials to say it is safe before going home. Avoid fallen power lines, poles and wires, because they can electrocute you. Watch out for falling trees and other debris.

Source: American Red Cross, Flood Preparedness Checklist (2022), PDF page 2.
Origin of the sources: US. Audience: public. Type: taken from the sources.

## FL-18 Cleanup safety: protective equipment and pacing

Flood cleanup safety: many injuries happen during cleanup. Wear protective equipment such as boots, long pants, work gloves, eyewear and an N95 respirator to protect your lungs, and follow the advice of local public health officials. Cleaning up is a big job: work with a partner and take frequent breaks.

Source: American Red Cross, Flood Preparedness Checklist (2022), PDF page 2.
Origin of the sources: US. Audience: public. Type: taken from the sources.

## FL-19 Drying a flooded home and preventing mold

Flooded home: if possible, dry the home and everything in it as quickly as you can, within 24 to 48 hours. If you cannot dry it within 24 to 48 hours, assume you have mold growth. When it is safe to return, dry everything completely, clean up the mold and make sure no moisture problem remains. Keep wet areas well ventilated and throw away wet materials that cannot be repaired or dried. A building that was flooded and not dried out within 24 to 48 hours is likely to develop mold, and time spent in such conditions should be limited.

Source: American Red Cross, Flood Preparedness Checklist (2022), PDF page 2; FEMA P-2055, Post-disaster Building Safety Evaluation Guidance (2019), PDF page 89.
Origin of the sources: US. Audience: public. Type: taken from the sources.

## FL-20 Mold health effects and where to look

Mold after flooding: floodwater wicks up drywall by capillary action and stimulates mold growth. Mold exposure can worsen asthma symptoms and cause allergic reactions, and persistent mold and dampness can cause coughing, wheezing, upper respiratory symptoms and shortness of breath. Persistent dampness inside walls, crawlspaces, attics or ventilation chases can support mold growth that might only be noticeable by a musty odor. If mold is suspected, evaluate the building from the outside only.

Source: FEMA P-2055, Post-disaster Building Safety Evaluation Guidance (2019), PDF pages 89, 90.
Origin of the sources: US. Audience: professional. Type: taken from the sources.

## FL-21 Carbon monoxide after a flood: generators, heaters, charcoal

Carbon monoxide after a flood: gasoline, propane, natural gas or charcoal-burning devices should never be used inside a home, basement, garage, tent or camper, or even outside near an open window. Portable generators should be kept outdoors and at least 20 feet from doors, windows or any other building opening. Carbon monoxide cannot be seen or smelled but can kill you fast. If you start to feel sick, dizzy or weak, get to fresh air right away and do not delay.

Source: American Red Cross, Flood Preparedness Checklist (2022), PDF page 2; FEMA P-2055, Post-disaster Building Safety Evaluation Guidance (2019), PDF page 86.
Origin of the sources: US. Audience: public. Type: taken from the sources.

## FL-22 Lighting during and after a flood: flashlights, not candles

Lighting during a power cut after a flood: use flashlights or battery-powered lanterns rather than candles, to reduce fire risk. The French emergency kit list includes a candle with matches or a lighter, which conflicts with this advice. The safer option is a flashlight with spare batteries, and ignition sources are to be avoided if you smell gas.

Source: American Red Cross, Flood Preparedness Checklist (2022), PDF page 2; Pluie et inondation, version FALC (Ministère français, adaptation Com'access), PDF page 6; FEMA P-2055, Post-disaster Building Safety Evaluation Guidance (2019), PDF page 86.

## FL-23 Emotional strain after a flood

After a flood it is normal to have a lot of bad feelings, stress or anxiety. Eat healthy food and get enough sleep to help deal with stress. The Red Cross lists a free disaster distress helpline, but its number is for the United States only and does not apply in Tunisia.

## FL-24 Chemicals, propane tanks and car batteries moved by floodwater

Hazards moved by floodwater: floodwater may bury or move industrial chemicals from their storage places, and residue chemicals may remain in porous materials such as drywall and wood. Propane tanks pose a danger of fire or explosion, and if any are found the local police or fire department or the authority having jurisdiction should be contacted immediately. Car batteries may still hold an electrical charge and spilled acid, so make no contact. Containers of dry chemicals that became wet can be dangerous, so notify the local fire department.

Source: FEMA P-2055, Post-disaster Building Safety Evaluation Guidance (2019), PDF pages 87, 88.
Origin of the sources: US. Audience: professional. Type: taken from the sources.

## FL-25 Flooded building: when it should be restricted or declared unsafe

Safety evaluation of a flooded building: standard practice is to post a RESTRICTED USE placard when there is evidence that the water level rose above the sill plate. In one training example, a flooded building with mold was posted RESTRICTED USE and entry was acceptable only for retrieving possessions, with personal protection against mold. After blackwater and contaminants are removed and repairs are completed by qualified contractors, the local authority may allow a certified contractor to change or remove the placard. This guidance comes from the United States and the authority and process differ by country.

Source: FEMA P-2055, Post-disaster Building Safety Evaluation Guidance (2019), PDF pages 90, 179.
Origin of the sources: US. Audience: professional. Type: taken from the sources.
Team note, not from the sources: This guidance comes from the United States and the authority and process differ by country.

## FL-26 Water may recede and rise again

Flood follow-up: floodwaters may recede and then rise again, and mold growth may be made worse in the meantime. A building evaluated after a flood may need to be evaluated again if a secondary event occurs.

Source: FEMA P-2055, Post-disaster Building Safety Evaluation Guidance (2019), PDF page 176.
Origin of the sources: US. Audience: public. Type: taken from the sources.
